Part 2

Signing in to apps

Part 1 covered keys, signatures and passkeys. This part covers the login process. The standards involved, OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect, are the plumbing of almost every modern login. IdentiKey conforms to both, so an Oauth-equipped app can offer "Sign in with IdentiKey" as easily as "Sign in with Google". The sections below start with what an identity provider is, then follow one sign-in, from the first redirect to the final token. It ends by presenting what identifier the app keeps to recognise you, and who controls it.

8. Identity providers, OAuth and OpenID Connect

Before delegated sign-in existed, every app kept its own password table. Every table was a target, every user reused passwords across them, and a breach at one leaked into the rest. Delegated sign-in takes authentication out of each app and gives it to a single external service, the identity provider, whose sole purpose is to authenticate users. The app, called the relying party (RP), sends you to the provider, which checks who you are and hands the app a signed statement saying so. The app never sees a credential, only the statement.

Two standards describe the choreography:

  • OAuth 2.0 was written for authorization, letting one service act on your behalf at another, and it supplies the moving parts: registered clients (apps the provider knows in advance), redirects (your browser is sent to the provider and back), short-lived codes (a one-time receipt for the sign-in, handed back with you), and a token endpoint where the app trades that code for an access token, a credential it will present as its proof of what it is allowed to do. Section 10 follows one sign-in through all four.
  • OpenID Connect (OIDC) is a thin identity layer on top of OAuth. It adds the ID token, a second kind of token that says who you are (versus an OAuth tokens description of what an app may do), and discovery, a URL at a standard, fixed path (/.well-known/...) where the provider publishes its endpoints, the address of its signing keys, and its supported options so an app can configure itself from one address.

OIDC has its own names for the pieces of a provider:

  • OpenID Provider (OP). The role IdentiKey plays: the service that authenticates people and issues signed statements about them. "Identity provider" is the general term; "OpenID Provider" is what OIDC calls one that speaks OIDC. The provider is a service with a database. IdentiKey's holds accounts (each an XID, its custody rung and a copy of its XID document), registered passkeys, a consent log and the hashes of live refresh tokens (registered clients are loaded from the service's configuration when it starts, and sign-in codes and access tokens are held only in memory); section 22 lists what is stored where. That database is operational state, not the source of your identity: the XID is defined by a key, the database holds a copy of the document that describes it, and only a key IdentiKey does not hold can change that document. Part 5 is about what survives if the database, or IdentiKey itself, goes away.
  • Issuer. A provider's name, written as a URL. At IdentiKey it is also the base every one of its endpoints hangs off. IdentiKey's is https://auth.identikey.me. Every path in this tutorial, /token, /userinfo and the rest, is relative to it. An app may accept sign-ins from more than one provider, so every ID token carries a field, iss, indicating which provider created it, recorded via its issuer URL when it signs the token. The app, on receiving the token, checks that the field holds an issuer it was configured to trust, and rejects the token if it does not.
  • Discovery document. The discovery document advertises what the provider will do. A JSON file every OP publishes at <issuer>/.well-known/openid-configuration. It lists the URLs of the provider's endpoints, the addresses that each do one job, and the options the provider supports. The three every app needs are the authorization endpoint, where the app sends your browser to sign in; the token endpoint, which the app calls afterwards to trade the one-time code it received for an ID token; and the JWKS endpoint, the signing keys below. Section 10 covers the first two in order. Given only the issuer URL, an app can fetch this file and configure itself, instead of a developer copying each endpoint URL into the app's settings by hand.
  • Signing keys. The public keys an app uses to verify the provider's signatures, published at /.well-known/jwks.json (a JSON Web Key Set). The private halves never leave the provider. Apps fetch this file and check every token against it.

The concepts above are the OIDC standard, common to every provider. What follows are the choices IdentiKey has made within that standard, as its own discovery document reports them. Each is stricter than OIDC requires.

Identikey allows an app to bring a user in by:

  • the standard browser flow (redirect to IdentiKey, sign in with a passkey; section 10)
  • a flow for devices with no browser, such as a TV (section 11)
  • IdentiKey's own key-based challenge, where a key the user holds signs a challenge and no sign-in page is involved (section 25)

Every browser sign-in must use PKCE (Proof Key for Code Exchange, a guard against stolen sign-in codes, explained in section 10), with no weaker option.

By default each app sees a different identifier for the same person, so two apps cannot match their users up.

ID tokens are signed with Ed25519 only; no RSA, no shared-secret HMAC, no choice.

And the only thing an app can ask for is proof of who you are, delivered as the ID token: no email, no profile.

(Each of these decisions comes up again in sections 10 to 12)

Before an app can offer "Sign in with IdentiKey", IdentiKey has to know the app exists. That is client registration: the provider records who the app is and where it is allowed to send users back to, and gives the app a client id to quote in every request. OIDC also defines a way for an app to register itself, by calling an endpoint with no human involved. IdentiKey has no such endpoint - an operator registers each app with the provider, so an unknown app cannot turn up and start sending people through the sign-in flow. Every relying party was admitted on purpose, and the provider knows exactly which addresses it may hand a code back to.

Each registration names

  • a client id
  • one or more exact redirect URIs (https, except loopback addresses for tools running on your own machine)
  • a sector (section 12)
  • the grants the client may use
  • and whether it is confidential or public.

A confidential client runs on a server and holds a secret, which is written in the service's configuration and which the provider hashes when it loads, keeping only the hash in memory. A public client runs where a secret could not be kept, such as a browser single-page app or a command-line tool, and relies on PKCE instead (explained in section 10). Any client may list extra redirect URIs, and the provider's CORS policy (the browser rule for which other sites' pages may call an endpoint) admits the origins of every registered client's redirect URIs to the token endpoint, including loopback with any port for local development.

The relying parties in production today are the Mjolnir command line and terminal (public clients), the Taskmaster web app (two confidential clients, one for production and one for a developer laptop), the World Tree site's content manager (a public single-page app), and a Matrix homeserver (Conduit, confidential). Each of them sees IdentiKey as an ordinary OIDC provider. None of them had to learn about XIDs or passkeys to integrate.

What the app gets by choosing IdentiKey over a "sign in with" button from a large platform comes down to three things that the rest of this tutorial explains:

  • the identifier the app receives is derived from the identity's own key, not an account number that only has meaning within the provider's database (Part 3)
  • the user authenticates with a passkey and no password exists anywhere (section 10)
  • and the provider is built so that the user could take their identity elsewhere (Part 5).

The point of the first and third is portability: an account number is only meaningful to the provider that issued it, while an identity defined by a key can outlive the provider and be proved to anyone. The point of the second is that there is nothing to steal: no password sits in a database waiting to be breached, and none can be typed into a fake sign-in page, because a passkey only answers the site it was made for (section 7).

9. Tokens and claims

A successful sign-in ends with the provider returning three tokens to the app. They look alike on the wire, as opaque strings, but they do different jobs:

The ID token is the identity statement. It is a JSON Web Token (JWT), three strings of encoded text (base64url) joined by dots. Everything the token says is in those strings; they are readable by anyone who decodes them. What stops anyone changing them is the signature (section 4): the provider signed the first two strings with its private key, so if a single character of the claims is altered, the signature no longer verifies against the provider's public key and the app rejects the token.

  • The first string is a header naming the algorithm (EdDSA) and a key id (kid).
  • The second string is a JSON object of claims, the statements the token makes.
  • The third string is the provider's signature over the first two.

IdentiKey's ID token carries only claims the OIDC standard defines, and no custom ones:

Claim Meaning
iss The issuer, https://auth.identikey.me.
sub The subject: the identifier by which this app will know the user. IdentiKey derives it from the user's XID, by default differently for each app. Section 12.
aud The audience: the client id of the app this token was made for. An app accepts a token only if aud names it, so a token issued to one app cannot be replayed at another.
iat, exp Issued-at and expiry. The lifetime is the provider's choice; IdentiKey sets it to one hour.
nonce A random value the app generated at the start of sign-in and stored in the user's session. The provider copies it into the ID token issued at sign-in (tokens issued later, on refresh or through the device grant, carry none); when the token arrives, the app compares the nonce inside it with the one it stored, and rejects the token if they differ. A token captured from some other sign-in carries the wrong nonce and so cannot be slipped into this one.

To verify an ID token, the app fetches the provider's JWKS (IdentiKey's own signing keys, one set for the whole service; users' keys are not in it, they live in each user's XID document, Part 3), finds the key whose kid matches the header, checks the signature with that public key, and then checks that iss is the issuer it expects, that aud is its own client id, that exp is in the future, and that nonce matches what it sent. The key id lets a provider publish more than one key at a time, so signing keys can be rotated without a flag day; IdentiKey publishes a single key today. The whole check happens locally; the app does not need to call the provider again.

The access token is a random string that means something only to the provider. It carries no claims. The provider remembers which subject and client it belongs to and lets it live for one hour. An app, or a service the app calls, can present it to /userinfo, which returns the subject, or to /introspect, which reports whether it is still active and for whom. Because the access token is opaque, nothing can be read from a stolen one without asking the provider, and the provider can refuse.

The refresh token lets the app obtain a new access token and ID token once the hour is up, by calling /token itself, so the user is not sent back to the sign-in page. It is also a random string. The provider, IdentiKey's hosted service at auth.identikey.me, stores only its SHA-256 hash in Postgres, so a database leak yields nothing redeemable. Every use rotates it: the provider issues a new refresh token and deletes the old hash in the same operation. A stolen refresh token therefore works at most once, and if an attacker uses it first, the legitimate app's next refresh fails, which is a detectable event rather than a silent one. The refresh token is bound to the client that received it, and /revoke deletes it on request.

Token Form Lifetime Who reads it
ID token signed JWT, EdDSA 1 hour the app, locally, by signature
access token opaque random string 1 hour the provider, via /userinfo or /introspect
refresh token opaque random string, stored hashed, rotates on use until revoked or rotated the provider's token endpoint

10. Sign-in, step by step

The standard OIDC flow for a browser app, and the first of the three in section 8, is authorization code with PKCE. The letters stand for Proof Key for Code Exchange, and the sequence is as follows: the app invents a secret at the start, sends only a hash of it, and reveals the secret at the end. Anyone who intercepts the middle of the flow holds a code they cannot redeem.

  1. The app prepares. It generates a random secret, the code_verifier (a long random string it keeps to itself), hashes it with SHA-256, and base64url-encodes the digest (the raw 32-byte result of running SHA-256 over the verifier) as the code_challenge (that digest as a 43-character string, sent to the provider with the login request so the verifier can be checked later). It also picks a random state (to match the response to this browser session) and a random nonce (to match the ID token). The app stores all three, verifier, state and nonce, in the user's browser session before it redirects, because it needs each of them when the browser comes back: it compares state and nonce itself, and sends the verifier to the provider at /token.

  2. The browser is sent to the provider. The app redirects to /authorize with response_type=code, its client_id, its redirect_uri, scope=openid, the state, the nonce, the code_challenge and code_challenge_method=S256.

    These travel as query-string parameters on the /authorize URL. The app answers with a 302 redirect, and the browser fetches that URL from the provider over HTTPS. So they show up in the address bar, browser history and server logs. That is harmless because none of them is secret: client_id, redirect_uri and scope are public, state and nonce are only echoed back and compared, and code_challenge is a hash. The secret code_verifier stays in the app's session (step 1) until step 7, where it travels in the body of the POST to /token. On the provider's side, the nonce and challenge are kept in the pending authorization of step 6.

  3. The provider checks the request. The client id must be registered, the redirect URI must match a registered one exactly (no prefix matching, no wildcard; the one exception is a loopback address, whose port may differ, as the standard allows for local apps), and the challenge method must be S256 (a request that names no method is treated as S256). A request with no client id is rejected before anything else happens. The exact match on the redirect URI is what stops an attacker from putting an address of their own in the request and having the code delivered there: the provider will only ever send a code to an address the app registered in advance (section 8). S256 is required because the only alternative the standard allows, plain, sends the verifier itself as the challenge and so gives the secret away at the start.

  4. The user authenticates with a passkey. The provider redirects to its hosted /authorize/passkey page, which asks the browser for a passkey assertion for the relying-party id auth.identikey.me (section 7). The browser or password manager prompts for Face ID, a fingerprint or a PIN, and returns a signed assertion. The provider verifies the signature against the public key it stored at registration, checks the counter and the origin, and now knows which account is present. The counter is the signature counter of section 7, which, if the authenticator keeps one (synced passkeys report zero every time), must be higher than the last value the provider saw for this passkey. The origin is the web address the browser itself writes into the assertion to say where the signing happened, and the provider accepts it only if it is the provider's own, https://auth.identikey.me. Because the assertion is bound to the provider's domain, a lookalike site cannot obtain one.
  5. If the passkey is unknown, the provider shows a recovery page offering to try another passkey or, as an explicit and separate choice, to create a new account. An unrecognised credential never creates an account by itself.
  6. The provider issues a code. It records a pending authorization (which account, which client, the redirect URI, the nonce and the code challenge) under a single-use code that lives ten minutes, and redirects the browser back to the app's redirect URI with code and state.
  7. The app redeems the code. After confirming that state matches what it sent, it POSTs to /token with grant_type=authorization_code, the code, the same redirect URI, its client_id and the original code_verifier. A confidential client also presents its secret, in an HTTP Basic header or in the form body.
  8. The provider checks the proof. It hashes the verifier, compares it to the stored challenge, authenticates the client if it is confidential (it hashes the secret the client presented and compares the result with the hash it stored at registration, section 8), confirms the client's registration lists the code grant, confirms the code was issued to this client, and confirms the redirect URI presented now is the same one the code was sent to in step 6. The code is consumed; presenting it again fails, and if the code had already been redeemed, the access token issued the first time is revoked as well (the refresh token issued with it is not), on the assumption that one of the two presenters is an attacker. The provider mints the three tokens of section 9.
  9. The app verifies the ID token (the checks listed in section 9) and creates its own session. From here on the app's cookie, not the provider's, keeps the user signed in to the app.

Two of these checks deserve a word on what they stop. The nonce stops a captured ID token being replayed into a different sign-in, but it says nothing about which client redeemed the code, so the code itself is bound to the client id and cannot be redeemed by another registration. PKCE stops a code captured in transit, for example by a malicious app registered for the same custom URL scheme on a phone, from being exchanged by anyone who does not hold the verifier. (A native app on a phone often registers a redirect URI under a scheme of its own, such as myapp://callback, and the operating system hands anything addressed to that scheme to whichever installed app claimed it; a second app claiming the same scheme would receive the code, but not the verifier.) For a public client, which has no secret, PKCE is the only thing binding the exchange to the party that started it, which is why IdentiKey makes it mandatory.

One implementation detail is worth knowing because it shows up in network logs. The passkey page completes the ceremony with a fetch call, and a browser fetch hides the destination of a redirect from the page's script. So the provider's passkey-finish endpoint returns the redirect URL as JSON, and the page navigates to it. The result is the same as step 6, but you will see a JSON response rather than a 302 (the HTTP status code a server sends to tell the browser to go to another address).

11. Signing in without a browser

A command-line tool over SSH, or a program on a machine with no display, cannot run the redirect dance of section 10, which needs a browser that can be sent to the provider's sign-in page and back to the app again. The device authorization grant (RFC 8628) splits the flow across two devices: the one that wants the tokens and the one that has your passkey.

  1. The tool POSTs its client id to /device_authorization. The provider returns a device_code (a long secret for the tool: it is the only thing that ties the tool's later polls to this particular request, so it must be unguessable), a user_code (a short code for a human), the verification URL /device, a version of that URL with the code filled in, an expiry of fifteen minutes and a polling interval of five seconds.
  2. The tool shows the user code and URL, and starts polling /token with grant_type=urn:ietf:params:oauth:grant-type:device_code and the device code. Until the user acts, the provider answers authorization_pending, or slow_down if the tool polls too fast. The tool has to poll because it has no browser for the provider to redirect back to; the interval returned in step 1 is the provider saying how often it is willing to be asked.
  3. On a phone or laptop, the user opens /device, and types the code. The page shows which client is asking, by the name in that client's registration (section 8), and the user approves or denies; approving asks for a passkey exactly as in section 10. Only on approval does the provider mark the pending request as authorized for the account that just signed in.
  4. The tool's next poll returns the tokens of section 9, or access_denied, or expired_token if fifteen minutes passed.

The user code is eight letters drawn from twenty consonants, so it never spells a word and never contains characters that are easy to confuse when read aloud. The device code never leaves the tool. The user never sees it and the approval page never asks for it; the tool sends it only to /token, where the provider uses it to look up the pending request and, once that request has been approved, to release the tokens to the tool that started it.

The device grant is opt-in per client. That is, a client's registration (section 8) must list the device grant among the grants the client may use before the provider will accept its request at /device_authorization. Its weakness is social: an attacker can start a device flow, send you the user code with a plausible story, and if you approve it they hold your tokens. Nothing in the protocol can prevent this, because the device that asks and the device that approves are deliberately separate, and the only thing linking them is the code the user types; the provider cannot tell whether the person typing it is also the person who started the flow. So the grant is enabled only for registrations that need it, the Mjolnir command line being the case in production, and withheld from web apps that have a browser available. A client whose registration omits the grant gets unauthorized_client if it tries: the provider checks the registration at step 1, before it issues any codes.

12. Who the app sees: the subject

The sub claim is what the app stores as "this user". Everything else about the sign-in is transient; the subject is permanent, so the choice of what it is shapes privacy and portability for as long as the account exists.

OIDC defines two subject types. A public subject is the same string at every app, which is convenient and lets any two apps compare notes about who their users are. A pairwise subject is different for every app, so that two apps cannot discover they share a user by comparing subjects.

IdentiKey defaults to pairwise and derives the subject with HKDF, a standard key-derivation function (RFC 5869) built on HMAC-SHA-256. HMAC is a hash computed with a secret key mixed into the input, so nobody without the key can compute or predict its output; a key-derivation function uses that to turn one secret plus some labels into fresh bytes that look random yet come out the same every time for the same inputs. The inputs here are:

input key material: the deployment's pairwise salt (32 random bytes)
salt: the client's registered sector (for example "taskmaster.dev")
info: "identikey-oidc-sub-v1" || 0x00 || the user's XID (32 bytes)
output: 32 bytes, encoded base58btc

The three inputs are HKDF's standard ones. The input key material is the secret; the salt separates one use of that secret from another, here one app from another; and the info string labels what the output is for, here the subject scheme by version and the user by XID. Changing any one of the three changes every output byte, which is what the properties below rely on.

Three properties fall out of this construction:

  • Stable. The input is the user's XID (Part 3), which never changes, not a key, which does. The same person at the same app gets the same subject across every key rotation.
  • Unlinkable. Two apps with different sectors get unrelated subjects for the same person. Nobody holding a subject can compute the XID from it, nor one app's subject from another's, without the salt.
  • Not the XID. The pairwise subject is the same alphabet (base58btc, the text encoding section 13 describes: 58 letters and digits, with the look-alike characters 0, O, I and l left out) over different bytes. It never equals a XID and should not be mistaken for one.

The sector is usually the host of the app's redirect URI (taskmaster.dev, worldtree.network, or 127.0.0.1 for a developer's laptop). This follows the OIDC rule for pairwise subjects, which takes the host of the redirect URI as the sector identifier, so the subject is tied to the party the provider actually sends users back to. It is pinned explicitly in each client's registration, and a deploy-time check refuses a registration whose pin does not match the sector derived from its redirect, so a change to a redirect URI cannot silently change every subject at that app.

A client may instead be registered with a public subject, in which case sub is the base58btc XID itself. The Mjolnir clients use this: there the user wants the identifier they publish and the identifier the system checks ownership against to be the same string, and the ownership check is a plain string comparison against the XID.

The pairwise salt is created once per deployment and is never rotated, because rotating it would change every pairwise subject at once and sever every user from every account they hold at every app. It is held as an exportable operator secret, a value in the deployment's configuration that the operator can read out and carry to another deployment, rather than sealed in hardware that would never give it back, so that, if the provider ever had to be moved, the subjects could move with it. This is the first place in the plumbing where a principle from Part 5 shows up: a user should be able to leave, and nothing about how the system identifies them should make that impossible.

Subject type What sub is Used by What it protects
pairwise (default) base58btc of 32 HKDF bytes from salt, sector and XID Taskmaster, World Tree, Conduit apps cannot correlate users by subject
public base58btc of the XID Mjolnir command line and terminal the identifier is the one the user publishes

Putting Parts 1 and 2 together: one sign-in, end to end

An example: signing in. Sam has an IdentiKey identity and wants to sign in to an app. The pieces from Parts 1 and 2, in action:

The key pair:

  • When Sam created his identity, he registered a passkey. His device generated a key pair: a private key, which stays inside the device's secure hardware and never leaves it, and a public key, which the device sent to IdentiKey once, at that moment. IdentiKey stored the public key against Sam's account. This is Sam's credential in the sense of section 1.
  • Nothing secret crossed the network then, and nothing secret ever will. IdentiKey holds only the public key, which can check Sam's signatures but cannot produce one.

The challenge:

  • The app sends Sam to IdentiKey to sign in. IdentiKey generates a challenge: a fresh random number (a nonce), together with its own name as the intended audience and a short expiry. The challenge is the data that will be signed. IdentiKey keeps its own copy, so that when the signed response comes back it can check that the response answers exactly the challenge it issued and refuse a response to any other challenge.
  • Because IdentiKey invented the challenge a moment ago and will accept an answer to this one only, a signature over it proves that the key is in use right now, not that someone recorded a signature last week. The browser wraps the challenge, together with the origin of the page, in a small record called the client data and hashes that record with SHA-256; the authenticator signs that hash together with its own authenticator data, and the passkey's signature scheme (ES256, ECDSA on the P-256 curve, for most passkeys) hashes once more internally (section 4).

The signature:

  • Sam's device shows the passkey prompt. He touches the fingerprint sensor or looks at the camera, which unlocks the private key for a single use. This is the fingerprint-or-face unlock of section 7, called user verification: a local gate in front of the key, performed by the authenticator. IdentiKey requires it and sees a flag saying it happened, but never the fingerprint, face or PIN itself. The device's authenticator hands the private key and the challenge to its cryptography library, which runs the signing algorithm and returns a signature. The private key does not move; only the signature leaves the device.
  • The device sends the signature back to IdentiKey as the response, along with the credential id, so IdentiKey knows which stored public key to use. The response also carries the signature counter and the origin the browser saw; IdentiKey checks that the origin is its own and, when the authenticator keeps a counter, that it has advanced, as in step 4 of section 10. That one tap is the act of authentication from section 1.

Verification:

  • IdentiKey's server looks up the public key stored for that credential, takes the challenge it issued, and runs the verification algorithm on those two and the signature. It answers yes only if the private key matching the stored public key signed exactly this challenge.
  • A yes proves two things. Origin: whoever holds the private key registered to Sam's account was present just now. Integrity: the response answers this challenge and no other, so a replayed or altered response fails. It does not prove that the key is Sam's in any human sense; it proves that it is the key Sam registered, which is the only "Sam" IdentiKey knows.
  • IdentiKey then signs a statement of its own, the ID token (section 9), saying that this account authenticated, and hands it to the app. Concretely, the hand-over is the code exchange of section 10: the provider sends the browser back to the app with a single-use code, and the app redeems that code at /token for the ID token. The app verifies that signature with IdentiKey's published public key, fetched from the JWKS endpoint (section 8), and then checks the issuer, audience, expiry and nonce as section 9 lists. Signing happens twice in every sign-in: once by Sam, to prove himself to IdentiKey, and once by IdentiKey, to vouch for Sam to the app.

How this maps to the one-line definition of IdentiKey at the top of the document: "gives each person one identity" is the key pair and the identifier built from it (Part 3); "lets them prove they hold it" is signing the challenge; "hands the result of that proof to the app" is the ID token.
