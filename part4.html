Part 4

The hosted service

Parts 2 and 3 described IdentiKey from the outside, as an app or a verifier sees it. This part goes inside the hosted service at auth.identikey.me: what an account is made of, which keys exist and who holds them, how the service is allowed to use a key on your behalf, and what you can do from the account page. It is the most technical part of the tutorial, and it is also the part that matters most for understanding what IdentiKey actually is, because the honest answer to "who holds my keys?" is in the details.

17. Custody and access are two different questions

Two questions are easy to run together, and keeping them apart is the single most useful habit this tutorial can teach.

Custody asks: who can produce a signature with your key? If the answer is "only me", you have self-custody. If the answer is "the service, whenever it likes", you have a traditional account, however the login screen is dressed up. If the answer is "the service, but only when I have just proved I am present and asked for it, and it writes that down where I can check", you have something in between, and that something is what IdentiKey calls managed custody.

Access asks: how do you authenticate in order to reach the key? A password, an emailed link, a passkey, a hardware signer. These are mechanisms, not places on the custody scale. A passkey is not a kind of custody. Self-custody is not a login method. You can hold your own key and reach it with a passkey, or let a service hold your key and reach it with the same passkey.

Part 5 lays out both axes as a ladder and a grid. For this part, one line matters: the difference between a service that can sign without you and one that cannot sign without an authenticated instruction that it logs and you can audit. IdentiKey calls the first C1, custodial, and the second C2, managed, and the whole design of the hosted service is an attempt to build the second rather than the first. Sections 18 to 20 show how, and section 26 says how far it gets.

The numbers are positions on a scale IdentiKey calls custody rungs, C0 to C4, each answering the custody question differently; section 23 lays out the whole ladder, and this part needs only C1 and C2.

18. What signup creates

When you create an account at /signup, the service does the following, in one request, before you have touched a passkey.

  1. It generates two key pairs. The first is the user key, a PrivateKeyBase from the Blockchain Commons library (the open-source project that publishes the XID and Gordian Envelope specifications of Part 3, and the libraries that implement them): a Schnorr signing key on the secp256k1 curve together with an X25519 key for encryption. That bundle is the library's standard shape for an identity's key, which is why the inception key is Schnorr rather than Ed25519. The second is the operational key, an Ed25519 signing key that belongs to IdentiKey.

    X25519 is a key-agreement scheme, not a signature scheme: two parties each combine their own private key with the other's public key and arrive at the same shared secret, from which an encryption key is derived. It is what lets other people encrypt data to you (section 21).

  2. It builds the XID document. The user key is the inception key, so the XID is the hash of its public half. In the document the user key holds Elect, Transfer, Verify and Update: the governing privileges from section 14. The operational key holds Sign and Auth: the doing privileges. The construction is refused if it would give IdentiKey's key All or Elect, or would otherwise fail the shape of a managed document. (All is the privilege that stands for every other, so granting it would be granting Elect by another name.)

    That shape is the one section 17 asked for: the key IdentiKey holds carries only the doing privileges, and every governing privilege sits on a key IdentiKey does not hold. The check runs while the document is being built, before anything is stored, so an account that breaks the rule is never created.

  3. It discards the user's signing private key. The Schnorr private key is dropped from memory. It is never written to the database. Nothing in the service can sign with the key that holds Elect.

  4. It seals two secrets it will keep. The user key's X25519 private scalar is sealed as the encapsulation key (section 21 explains what it is for). The operational key's thirty-two-byte Ed25519 seed is sealed as the managed key blob. Both are encrypted with XChaCha20-Poly1305 under the deployment's managed wrap key, a thirty-two-byte secret the server receives from its environment and that is identified by a key id so it can be rotated forward. The sealed envelope binds the account's XID and the key's public half, so a blob cannot be moved from one account to another.

    A scalar is simply the private half of an X25519 key: a single thirty-two-byte number. XChaCha20-Poly1305 is an authenticated encryption scheme: it hides the contents and attaches a tag that fails to verify if a single byte of the sealed data is altered, so a blob cannot be tampered with undetected, and because the XID and the public half are bound into the envelope, a blob presented under any other account fails to open rather than yielding a key. The key id is stored with each blob so that, when a new wrap key is introduced, the server can still tell which key opens an older blob.

  5. It stores the account with custody rung c2, the document, and the two sealed blobs.

  6. It signs you in and starts passkey registration. The browser receives an identikey_account cookie (HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite=Lax, valid twenty-four hours) that identifies this account to the service, and the WebAuthn registration options of section 7 with the XID as the user name. Until you save a passkey the account exists but cannot be reached from another browser.

    The three cookie flags each close one door: HttpOnly keeps the cookie out of reach of scripts running in the page, Secure sends it only over HTTPS, and SameSite=Lax withholds it from requests other sites make in the background or by form post, sending it only when you navigate to the service. At this moment the cookie is the only credential the account has, and it lives in this browser alone, which is why no other browser can reach the account yet.

The result is an account whose document has exactly the shape of managed custody: the signing key IdentiKey holds cannot change the key graph (the set of keys the document lists and the privileges each holds), and the key that can change the key graph is not IdentiKey's. It is also an account in which, at this moment, nobody holds the private half of the Elect key at all. The service threw it away on purpose, because keeping it would have made the privilege split a promise rather than a mechanism (section 14). What that absence means for the custody rung is the subject of section 26.

19. Consent and the log

If IdentiKey holds the signing key, what stops it signing whenever it likes? Code, and a record. The code path that uses the operational key requires a fresh proof from you (section 20). The record is the consent log, and it is what makes the claim auditable rather than merely asserted.

The consent log is a Postgres table that refuses UPDATE and DELETE. Each row describes one use, or attempted use, of your key, and the rows form a hash chain: every entry includes the hash of the previous entry, and its own hash is computed over a fixed, length-prefixed encoding of its fields. Change or remove any row and every later hash stops matching. The fields committed by the hash are:

The refusal is a rule of the table itself, not of the application code, so a row cannot be altered after it is written by anything the application's queries do (only removing the rule itself, a change to the database's schema, would allow it). Length-prefixing each field is what makes the encoding unambiguous: without it, two different sets of field values could run together into the same bytes and so the same hash.

Field What it records
xid, seq, prev_hash Which account, the position in that account's chain, and the link to the previous entry.
action What was done: signing_instruction_authorized, signing_completed, signing_failed, a device added, an encapsulation_export, encapsulation_unwrap or encapsulation_rewrap, or a custody migration.
audience, purpose Who the signature was for and the human-readable reason shown to you. The purpose is in the hash so that what the log says you agreed to cannot be edited after the fact.
access_level How you proved presence: A3 for a passkey, A4 for a signature from a key you hold.
credential_id Which passkey or device credential made the proof.
proof_kind, proof_hash The kind of proof (a WebAuthn assertion or an identikey-auth Response) and the SHA-256 of its bytes. The bytes themselves are stored beside the row.
result signed, refused or error.

One design choice here is subtle and central. The proof in each row is an access credential: a WebAuthn assertion your authenticator produced, or a response signed by a key only you hold. It is never a signature made with the managed key. If the log recorded IdentiKey's own signatures as evidence of consent, it would be IdentiKey vouching for IdentiKey, and the rung would be theatre. Because the proof is something only your authenticator could have made, each row is evidence that you were there.

What the log gives you is an answer to "what has been signed in my name, when, for whom, and on what proof?" that the service cannot quietly rewrite. It does not, by itself, stop a dishonest operator from ignoring the code and signing off the books: an operator holds the managed wrap key, and could in principle unseal the operational seed outside the ceremony and sign without writing a row. No log can. What it does is make the honest path and the dishonest path leave different evidence, which is the most a custodian can offer and more than a custodial account does.

20. Using the managed key: the challenge ceremony

The one thing IdentiKey's operational key does for you is answer challenges. A challenge is a small signed-request object from the identikey-auth protocol: canonical CBOR (dCBOR, section 14) carrying a version number, a random nonce, an audience naming the party asking, an issue time and an expiry. Answering it means signing it, producing a Response that the asking party can verify against your published document. This is how a Mjolnir server, for instance, confirms that the person at the keyboard is the owner of a given XID before it binds a session to them.

Because the key that signs is IdentiKey's, the service wraps every use of it in a ceremony that needs you in the loop:

  1. Begin. The client sends POST /managed-challenge/begin with your XID, the challenge bytes (at most 8 KB, with an expiry no more than ten minutes out and a two-minute allowance for clock skew), a commitment to a secret the client will reveal later (the same PKCE idea as section 10), which kind of proof you will give (a3 for a passkey, a4 for a key you hold), and the details of the session being bound: an Ed25519 session public key, an operation and a resource (each a short tuple of printable components), and a thirty-two-byte channel identifier. The service stores a pending attempt and returns a request id and a consent challenge: a digest, under the domain tag identikey-managed-consent-v1, over everything just stored. It is valid for five minutes.

    The session details are there because the ceremony produces two signatures, not one: besides answering the challenge, the operational key signs a statement that this session key, for this operation on this resource, belongs to you, which is the bind signature of steps 3 and 4. And because the consent challenge is a digest over every stored field, what you sign in the next step is tied to exactly this challenge, this session and this operation; nothing can be swapped between begin and finish.

  2. Prove. You answer the consent challenge. With a3, your browser produces a passkey assertion (over WebAuthn's own random challenge, which the service has tied to this attempt), sent to /managed-challenge/finish/a3. With a4, a key you hold signs it, sent to /finish/a4. A wallet acting as the a4 signer recomputes the digest from the returned fields and refuses to sign if they do not match, so a client cannot show you one thing and have you consent to another. The a4 path needs an Ed25519 key that holds Elect in the document, which an account made at hosted signup does not have (its Elect key is the inception key, Schnorr and discarded), so for those accounts a3 is the only way through today.

  3. Sign, once. The service verifies your proof. Then, in a database transaction of its own, it rechecks that the consent has not expired and claims the key use so it can never be repeated. Only once that claim is committed does it go on: it unseals the Ed25519 seed from the managed key blob, signs the stored challenge to make the Response, signs the session bind with the same key, writes the consent-log entries of section 19, and zeroizes the seed. If signing fails, a signing_failed entry is written and nothing is released. A proof that does not verify, or a consent that has expired, is refused before anything is claimed and writes no entry; and if the outcome cannot be saved after signing, the attempt is left claimed and marked indeterminate when the service next starts.

    Because the claim is committed, under a lock on the attempt, before the key is unsealed, two requests that race to finish the same attempt cannot both be served: one claims it, the other finds it already claimed. Zeroizing means overwriting the seed's bytes in memory with zeros before the memory is released, so the unsealed key does not linger where a later bug or a memory dump could find it.

  4. Retrieve. The client calls /managed-challenge/retrieve with the secret it committed to at begin. If the attempt completed and the secret matches, it receives the Response and the bind signature together. A missing or wrong secret receives neither. The service stores five states for an attempt: authorized (the proof was accepted), claimed, completed, failed, and indeterminate when the outcome could not be established. Retrieve reports an attempt that has not finished, including one whose proof has not yet arrived, as pending.

    The secret is what ties the result to the client that began the ceremony: anyone who learned the request id, from a log or a shared screen, cannot collect the Response without it.

Three boundaries deserve emphasis. The account cookie alone cannot start a signature; only a fresh assertion or a fresh signature can. The service signs exactly the bytes you consented to and does not consume or evaluate the asking party's nonce or policy, which stay the asking party's responsibility. And a signature made through this ceremony is an A3 (or A4-authorized) use of a managed key. It is never counted as the user having signed with a key of their own.

The mental model is a notary who holds a stamp with your name on it, uses it only while you stand in front of them and say what to stamp, stamps each thing exactly once, and writes the entry in a bound ledger with your own signature beside it.

21. The account page, passkeys and devices

GET /account, with your account cookie, shows what the service knows about you: your XID, your custody rung, and the passkeys enrolled on the account, each with its provider's icon where known, whether it is backed up to a cloud keychain, and when it was last used. The page is deliberately plain. The XID is the only name the service has for you, and there is no email, no username and no profile to fill in.

Adding a passkey requires the account cookie for that XID, obtained by signing in with a passkey you already have. A XID by itself is public information and lets nobody add anything. A passkey from a browser the account has never seen is simply unknown, and signing in with it leads to the recovery page of section 10 rather than to any account. Both rules follow from one principle the service is built on: nothing about the account should be reachable with less than the account's own credential.

SSH and git keys can be registered as devices at /devices/ssh_git. You send the public key only, with an optional nickname, and the request must carry a fresh passkey assertion; a session that came from an ordinary OIDC login is refused. The key appears as a credential of kind ssh_git on the account and can be revoked. The private key never leaves your machine.

The refusal follows the principle above. A device key, once listed, is a credential of the account, so adding one needs the account's own credential used freshly; an OIDC session proves only that a sign-in happened at some earlier moment.

The encapsulation key is the sealed X25519 half of your user key from section 18. It exists so that data can be encrypted to you by anyone who can read your document (the X25519 public half is published in it, which is how a sender finds the key), using IdentiKey's own construction from standard parts (X25519 key agreement, a BLAKE3 key derivation and XChaCha20-Poly1305 encryption), and so that shared secrets inside a group (a household, a team) can list you as a recipient. Three operations touch it, all at /account/encapsulation/:

  • begin names the purpose, export, unwrap or rewrap, and returns a fresh challenge with a five-minute expiry. It needs the account cookie and, for unwrap and rewrap, the exact wrap bytes, whose digest is stored so that consent for one wrap cannot open another.

    A wrap, here, is a data key (the symmetric key that actually encrypts some content) encrypted to one recipient's X25519 public key. unwrap opens such a wrap for you; rewrap re-addresses one to someone else. The hosted service offers export and rewrap; unwrap exists in the key library but has no route on the service yet.

  • export returns the X25519 private scalar to you after a fresh passkey assertion or a signature from a key you hold. The export is written to the consent log, and the service keeps its copy until a later custody migration, which the companion Spec document describes, checks that record and destroys it.

  • rewrap takes a data key wrapped to you and returns it wrapped to a friend, named by XID. The friend's X25519 public is read from their stored document, never supplied by the client. The data key and your scalar are decrypted inside the service and zeroized before the call returns; only the new wrap comes back. A returned wrap cannot be recalled, because once the friend holds it only their key is needed to open it: stopping that friend means choosing a new data key.

Finally, recovery. If you lose your passkey and have no other, the service cannot help you back into the account, and the recovery page says so: a new account will not restore the old one. This is not an oversight. An emailed link that could restore access to a key would make the email provider the real holder of the key, and Part 5 names the rule that forbids it. Having more than one passkey, on more than one device or backed up to a keychain, is the account recovery story.

22. Where every key lives

The table collects every key or secret the hosted service touches, so that the question "who holds what?" has one answer in one place.

Key or secret Algorithm Who holds the private half What it does
User signing key (inception) Schnorr, secp256k1 nobody; discarded at signup Hashed into the XID. Holds Elect, Transfer, Update, Verify in the document.
User encapsulation key X25519 IdentiKey, sealed under the managed wrap key; exportable to you Recipient for data encrypted to you. Export, unwrap and rewrap on consent.
Operational key Ed25519 IdentiKey, sealed under the managed wrap key Holds Sign and Auth. Answers challenges in the section 20 ceremony.
Passkey P-256 or RSA (ES256 or RS256), inside your authenticator you A3 proof of presence to IdentiKey. Consent artifact for every managed signature.
SSH or git device key any (the service does not check the algorithm) you Listed on the account as a credential.
Managed wrap key XChaCha20-Poly1305 key, kid v1 IdentiKey, from the server environment Seals the operational seed and the encapsulation scalar.
OIDC signing key Ed25519 IdentiKey, from its environment, or a file in its data directory if none is set Signs ID tokens. Published in the JWKS.
Pairwise salt 32 random bytes IdentiKey, from its environment, or a file in its data directory if none is set; an exportable operator secret Derives pairwise subjects. Never rotated.
Session key (Mjolnir bind) Ed25519, ephemeral the client program Bound to you by the bind signature in the challenge ceremony.

The pairwise salt is never rotated because every pairwise subject (section 12) is derived from it; a new salt would change the identifier that every app has stored for every user.

Two rows carry the weight of Part 5. The operational key is why IdentiKey can act for you, and the ceremony and log are what constrain that. The inception key's row, with nobody in the holder column, is why the account is not yet what its document says it is.
